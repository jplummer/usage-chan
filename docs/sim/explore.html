Reset anchor & overage

Two open questions from a single evening of poking at it, not a redesign: where the window's reset time lives, and what the screen does once the account starts spending real money. Nothing on this page is committed to screens.js or src/ui.cpp — it's candidates in dashboard-variants.js, compared against the shipped dashboard.

1 · Reset time, anchored at the top bar

Today the 5-hour block's reset time only ever shows up as the big headline (once under 40% left) or the small footnote (above it) — never in one fixed place. The 7-day bar already anchors it top-right on its label row; these anchor the 5-hour block's label row the same way. The catch: that slot is claimed by the big headline today, so anchoring it for real costs about one small-text row of height per block, against the "no title stripe" reclaim. Each treatment spends that cost differently — see which one still feels worth it.

2 · Getting into paid overage

The device already reads overage-status, overage-utilization, overage-reset and overage-disabled-reason on every fetch and shows none of it — see "Overage" in docs/design-decisions.md. Nobody has captured what these look like mid-overage yet, so these lean on a status word and an optional clock, never a dollar figure the header probe can't give them. Mark literally builds the parked idea from that doc — a mark past the bar's right edge, where the geometry already says past here it costs money. Badge leaves the bar alone and states the fact beside it. State treats overage as a different mode: pace stops meaning anything once you're past the wall, so the diamond and the "behind/ahead" caption drop out entirely.

3 · Seven-day pace diamond, off the straddle

Shipped draws the diamond at a fixed size centred on the 7-day track no matter how thin that track is — 8px of bar under a ~14px mark reads as the diamond stabbing through the bar rather than sitting on it. Proportional shrinks the diamond to the track's own scale, still centred through it. Atop keeps the track at its shipped thickness — it stays one clean, unbroken bar — and lifts the diamond off it entirely, tip resting on the top edge, so it reads as a marker floating above the timeline rather than a mark cut into it.

Uses the section 1 sliders — 7-day utilization and elapsed drive the diamond's position here too.

4 · The overage bar, vertical

The bar's rule everywhere else is fill is what's available — green grows from the bottom and shrinks as you spend. Shipped breaks that rule the moment overage starts: it floods the track solid, which under the same rule means everything remains, the exact opposite of what overage means. The three alternates keep the rule and differ only in what is genuinely left to draw. The track stops at 224 in every state so the layout never twitches, and the token straddles the track's end edge — the boundary you just crossed.

Only one of these can be built today. Blocked needs nothing but overage-status: rejected, which is already on the wire. Capped needs overage-utilization to be a real fraction of a real ceiling, and needs the overage window to reset with the window it belongs to so the diamond can be placed — neither has ever been observed. Uncapped is the fallback for when it turns out there is no denominator: nothing constrains you, so nothing is missing from the vessel, and pace retires rather than assert something false about an unbounded budget.

Drag it to zero and watch the token cover the last of the fill — the two want the same end of the bar, which is the cost of straddling the boundary.

5 · What the big caption says

Shipped swaps the headline for the reset time below 40% remaining. That was the right call when the headline and the footnote were neighbouring rows and only one of them could be prominent — the question changes from should I start this? to should I wait?, so the answer to the new question takes the big slot. In this layout the reset time has a permanent home at the bottom, which is the whole reason the bar stops at 224. The swap empties that home exactly when the clock matters most. Drag remaining below 40 on the left cell and watch the bottom row go blank.

The ladder keeps the principle and drops the mechanism. Every fact stays in one place and emphasis escalates instead: the headline always states the budget's own condition, the bottom row always answers when you get more, and colour does the work the swap used to do.

The unresolved part is the word below 12%. nearly out is what shipped says and what design-decisions.md blesses — a claim of proximity rather than a fraction, which cannot be wrong by 2× the way about a third left was. But it measures 116px against a 102px slot, so it does not fit the headline as written. Keeping the number fits and stays precise, but says nothing shipped does not already say. Pick one and it wants writing back into the decision log.

100% left is 104px in a 102px slot, so the top of the range clips too — the circles would need 4px more room, or that one string needs shortening.

6 · The refresh indicator

The control column's third circle is the first one to mean anything. Until now the poll interval was invisible: the screen changed when it changed, and nothing said whether a number was four seconds old or four minutes. The old updated 47s ago line said it in words and cost a row, and it answered the wrong question – you don't want the age of the last reading, you want to know when the next one lands.

So the circle is a clock that fills. It starts empty at the moment of a fetch and closes clockwise from twelve as the interval runs down, which means a full ring is a fetch about to happen and an empty one is a fetch that just did. While the request is actually in flight the ring gives way to a spinner – eight spokes with one dark gap moving round them. Two states, one object, in one place.

There's a reason to make this visible beyond knowing the age of a number. Every poll is a real API call that spends a sliver of the budget the screen is reporting. A thing that spends something should be something you can watch, and the same object is where a refresh now tap will land once there's somewhere for it to go – see Refresh as a visible, tappable object in plan.md.

The zoom row is the part worth checking. All three circles in that column are drawn by the same test – a pixel is in when its centre falls inside a radius-16 circle – so a full ring is pixel-identical to the discs above it. An earlier cut drew the discs with fillRoundRect and the pie by hand, and the full ring came out 32 pixels fatter than its neighbours. Nobody would have named it; everyone would have seen it.

The frame draws the spinner's spokes in Figma's default grey, which isn't in the palette. They're dim here, matching the ring in the frame beside it. If the spinner should shout instead, it's one constant.

7 · The control column, and how loud it should be

The three circles started as solid discs standing in for icons, and they read as three lamps — the brightest thing on a screen whose whole job is a number and a bar. Drawing the real glyphs takes about five-sixths of that ink away by itself. What is left is a set of choices that are easier to look at than to argue about, which is what this section is for.

The spinner. Bars and dots use the same eight positions and the same moving gap. The dots carry slightly more ink — about 196 lit pixels against 143 — and far fewer edges. Which one is quieter in the corner of your eye is not something a count settles.

Signal strength. Ghosted keeps the whole figure at every level, outlining the bands the signal doesn't reach, so strength reads as how much of one shape is solid and the scale is on screen every time you look. Absent draws only the bands you have, which is how every phone does it: quieter, and quietest exactly when the news is worst. That inversion used to be an argument against it. It isn't any more, because the alarm moved — a link too weak to fetch through now shows as a failed refresh on the circle below, which says the thing you actually care about instead of a proxy for it. No-link keeps the full ghosted figure either way: a strike through nothing says nothing.

Failure. The circle goes solid with an X taken out of it — the loudest mark in the column, for the only thing there that is bad news. It holds for five seconds and then gives way to the countdown to the retry. Worth noticing what that means: a device failing every poll shows the X for five seconds in every three hundred and looks healthy for the rest, which is the stale-reading-that-looks-fresh failure this project exists to avoid. The five seconds are the announcement; the standing condition still needs somewhere to live.

Reading this page

why the anchor treatments grow the block
The headline's big font already overshoots upward into the label row's own vertical space — it only gets away with it because the label sits far left and the headline far right of the same row. A third thing in that row's right slot needs its own line, not a gap that happens to be free.
why the overage treatments avoid dollar amounts
The header probe (what's actually wired up in api.cpp) gives a status word and fractions, not currency. A dollar figure only exists on the JSON usage endpoint, which is a separate, undecided switch — see docs/data-inventory.md. Building the overage display around a number the device doesn't have yet would be the same mistake the old "about a third left" phrasing made, the other direction.
what none of this replaces
The real fix is still capturing what the five overage headers actually do once an account crosses the wall — logged since 2026-09-08, read never. These three are candidates for once that capture happens, not a substitute for it.
